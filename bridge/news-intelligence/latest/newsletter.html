US-Europe AI investment gap widens

What happened: Oxford Economics projects US corporate spending on equipment and facilities will rise 40% between 2021 and 2027, compared to 12% in the Eurozone. This growth is primarily driven by massive AI data center investments from major US tech firms.

Why it matters: The disparity suggests a potential long-term productivity and infrastructure advantage for the US, as European markets struggle to match the scale of AI-focused capital expenditure.

Our read: Our read is that this trend signals a deepening divergence in industrial competitiveness, potentially forcing European policymakers to reconsider their approach to tech-sector incentives.

Enterprise adoption of Anthropic tools plateaus

What happened: Data from 70,000 companies via Ramp shows enterprise spending on Anthropic's Fable 5 model has plateaued at 11% of total AI tool spend. Meanwhile, Anthropic's annualized revenue reached $65 billion in July.

Why it matters: While revenue is growing, the plateau in enterprise share suggests that Anthropic faces stiff competition in securing long-term corporate loyalty against incumbents like OpenAI.

Our read: Our read is that the enterprise market is becoming increasingly fragmented, with companies testing multiple frontier models rather than standardising on a single provider.

Nvidia in talks to back Perplexity AI

What happened: Nvidia is in active discussions to join a new equity round for Perplexity AI, which would value the search startup at over $30 billion. Perplexity recently reported annualized revenue exceeding $750 million.

Why it matters: This move highlights Nvidia's strategy of deepening its influence across the AI stack by investing in high-growth application layers beyond its core hardware business.

Our read: Our read is that Nvidia is positioning itself to capture value from the 'search-to-answer' shift in AI, ensuring its hardware remains the backbone of the most popular AI interfaces.

New Zealand proposes social media ban for under-16s

What happened: The New Zealand government has introduced the Online Safety Bill, which would ban under-16s from high-risk social media platforms and impose fines of up to 10% of global turnover for non-compliance.

Why it matters: The bill represents a significant regulatory attempt to curb online harm, though its passage is uncertain as coalition partners have publicly stated they will vote against it.

Our read: Our read is that this is a high-profile political signal rather than a near-term legislative certainty, reflecting global tensions between tech platforms and child safety advocates.
